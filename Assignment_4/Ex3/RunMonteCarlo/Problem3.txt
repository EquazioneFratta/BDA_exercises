DATASET
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Stocks (3):

  - GOOGL (Alphabet Inc.)
  - NVDA  (NVIDIA Corporation)

Market Factors (3):
  - SPY (S&P 500 ETF)
  - QQQ (Nasdaq 100 ETF)
  - DIA (Dow Jones ETF)

Data source:   StockAnalysis.com
Time range:    2021-06-03 to 2026-06-03 (5 Years, Daily)
Data points:   ~1255 trading days per series
Return window: 2-week sliding window (10 trading days)

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
METHODOLOGY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Parse CSV files (StockAnalysis.com format: yyyy-MM-dd, Close price = col 4)
2. Align all time series to the same date range using trimToRegion + fillInHistory
3. Compute two-week returns via sliding window of size 10
4. Fit Ridge regression (lambda=1e-4) per stock against factor returns
5. Run Monte Carlo simulation:
   (c)(d) Sample factors from Multivariate Normal Distribution (correlated)
   (e)    Sample each factor independently from its own Normal Distribution
6. Compute VaR and CVaR at the 5% quantile

Simulation parameters:
  - Total trials:  1,000,000
  - Parallelism:   100 seeds x 10,000 trials each

(e) Portfolio VaR & CVaR (Independent Factors):
    Total trials:      1,000,000
    VaR  (5% quantile): -0.058875
    CVaR (5% quantile): -0.077073
    Runtime:            6.9 seconds

    Comparison with (c):
    +-------------+----------+----------+
    | Method      | VaR (5%) | CVaR(5%) |
    +-------------+----------+----------+
    | Correlated  | -0.06176 | -0.08061 |
    | Independent | -0.05888 | -0.07707 |
    +-------------+----------+----------+

    Interpretation: The correlated model produces a higher (more negative) VaR
    and CVaR than the independent model. This is because SPY, QQQ, and DIA are
    positively correlated — when one drops, the others tend to drop too. The
    Multivariate Normal Distribution captures this co-movement, leading to more
    extreme joint losses. The independence assumption underestimates tail risk
    by approximately 4-5%, since it ignores the amplification effect of
    correlated market downturns.
